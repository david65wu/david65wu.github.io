CS 180 · Project 1

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in color decades before color film existed, by exposing three black and white glass plates of the same scene through red, green, and blue filters. Reconstructing the color photo means digitally re-stacking those three exposures which first have to be aligned since the plates rarely line up on their own.

David Wu

Overview

Each glass plate negative is a single tall grayscale image containing three stacked exposures, filtered blue, green, and red from top to bottom. Splitting each negative into thirds gives the B, G, and R channels; the pipeline then searches for the (x, y) displacement that best lines up the G and R channels with the B channel, using normalized cross-correlation (NCC) as the similarity score over a cropped interior region (borders are excluded so plate edges and scratches don't bias the search).

Part 1

Single-Scale Alignment

On the low-resolution JPEGs, an exhaustive search over every integer displacement in [−15, 15]² is cheap enough to run directly (no pyramid needed):

  1. Split the plate into three equal-height strips, filtered blue, green, and red from top to bottom.
  2. For green and red, try every integer shift in [−15, 15]² relative to blue.
  3. Score each candidate by NCC over the interior only. A 10% border cropped from each side so plate edges and scratches don't bias the result.
  4. Keep the best-scoring shift for each channel.
  5. Roll green and red by their best shifts and stack with blue into the RGB composite.

Part 2

Multi-Scale Pyramid Alignment

The full-resolution scans are far too large and their true displacements often far too big for a single exhaustive search over every possible shift. Instead, each channel is aligned coarse-to-fine through an image pyramid:

  1. Build a pyramid for each channel by repeatedly downsampling 2× (simple box averaging) until the shorter side is ≤ 400px.
  2. Score each candidate shift over the interior only (a 10% border cropped from each side) so plate edges and scratches don't bias the result, just like the single-scale search.
  3. At the coarsest level, exhaustively score every integer shift in [−15, 15]² and keep the best one.
  4. Move one level finer: double the previous shift to match the new resolution, then refine it with a small ±3px search around that estimate.
  5. Repeat the refine step at each successively finer level, up through full resolution.
  6. Apply the final shift to the original full-resolution channel and composite with the reference channel.

Because only the coarsest, smallest level ever does a wide search, this keeps every image under a minute to align. All 14 provided images below were aligned this way and are shown with their computed (dy, dx) offsets.

Does the scoring metric matter: NCC vs. L2?

Each candidate shift is scored over two cropped interior patches, a (the channel being tested, already rolled by the candidate shift) and b (the reference channel), each of length n pixels:

NCC(a, b) = Σi (ai − a)(bi − b) sqrt(Σi(ai − a)²) · sqrt(Σi(bi − b)²)
L2(a, b) = −sqrt(Σi (ai − bi)²)

where a and b are each patch's mean pixel value, and higher is a better match for both (NCC tops out at 1; L2 tops out at 0). NCC's mean-subtraction and normalization make it invariant to a brightness or contrast difference between channels; L2 compares raw intensities directly and has no such protection. Run across all 17 images with both metrics, they agree exactly on 13 and differ by at most 1 pixel on 3 more. For most of this dataset the choice barely matters. The exception is lesnaya_doroga.tif: NCC finds the correct green shift (37, −32), while L2 lands on (−246, 166) and produces a scrambled composite. The forest scene has a strong brightness difference between channels (bright canopy, dark trunks and shadow), and L2's unnormalized distance gets dominated by that brightness gap instead of the underlying structure which is exactly the failure mode NCC's normalization exists to prevent. NCC is used as the default metric throughout this project for that reason.

Part 4

Diagnosing and Fixing a Failure: Emir

Aligning green and red directly to blue (the default for every other image) leaves emir with a badly misaligned red channel that is off by over 200 pixels horizontally, producing a visible red ghost around the seated figure and his patterned robe.

Why does it fail, and why does swapping the reference channel fix it?

The robe's repeating floral pattern fools NCC when red is compared directly to blue: at the coarse pyramid level, several wrong alignments of the pattern score just as well as the correct one, and once a wrong match is picked, the finer levels can only nudge it by a few pixels, which does not fix it. Measuring NCC at the true alignment confirms why: red and blue only score 0.27 against each other, versus 0.71 (green–blue) and 0.76 (red–green). Red and blue are genuinely the least alike pair here, likely because the robe's saturated colors reflect very differently under those two filters. Aligning through green instead sidesteps the bad pairing entirely. Every other image aligns to blue by default; emir.tif is the one exception.
